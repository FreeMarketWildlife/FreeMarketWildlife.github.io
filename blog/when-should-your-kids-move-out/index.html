When Should You Kick Your Kids Out?

By Tanoshi · November 3, 2026

Parenting · Personal reflections · Independence

On my eighth birthday, my dad handed me a piece of paper. It said “June 10, 2018” and “move-out date.”

I was confused. “Dad, what does this mean?”

He explained that in ten years, after I graduated high school, I would need to move out into the world. I would need a job and a way to support myself. He hoped that as I got older, I would become excited about being responsible for myself, finding a wife, and building a family.

What he didn’t realize was that I was already excited. I started imagining that I could stay up every night and eat whatever I wanted. Freedom!

That was March 16, 2008. Neither of us knew how much would change before that date arrived. A few years after the housing crash, we lost the house and moved into an apartment. I moved out of that house sooner than either of us expected, but not in the way Dad meant.

My parents divorced, and I ended up in a one-bedroom apartment with my mom and my brother Matt. Mom had the bedroom, and Matt and I lived in the living room. We made it work. I loved playing video games, and honestly, I remember having fun.

The day eventually came when I moved out at 18. It wasn’t exactly June 10. It was a little later that summer. But I knew it was coming.

I think my dad was trying to prepare me rather than spring the idea on me at the last minute. I had watched my older brother Steven approach the same milestone, and I had years to think about what it would mean for me.

My first place was a wake-up call

I worked several jobs through middle school and high school, got my driver’s license, and saved around $10,000 by the time I moved out. I was able to rent a room from an older woman at church.

That first place was a weird experience. There were mice, trash, mold, and a neglected swimming pool that had become a mosquito breeding ground. I came home to notices about it on the front door. It was a rough introduction to living on my own.

By the end of that October, I moved into another rented room. The first place taught me what it can look like when a household doesn’t have its shit together. It also taught me that moving out doesn’t mean your first home will be perfect.

Why I’m thankful I was pushed out of the nest

When people hear that I moved out at 18, they sometimes say, “I’m sorry. That must have been so rough.” They ask if I have a bad relationship with my parents. No! I have a great relationship with my mom and dad, and I’m thankful they pushed me out of the nest.

Leaving home taught me to recognize everything that had been given to me that I wasn’t doing for myself. I started to understand how much work my mom put into laundry, dishes, making dinner, and grocery shopping.

All of those things had a learning curve. I had to figure them out. And I’m grateful I went through that learning period early rather than reaching a later point in life without knowing how to take care of myself.

What need is there for an 18-year-old man to live at home and have his underwear folded by his mother anymore? At some point, he needs to wake up, smell the roses, and experience what it’s like to be out there in the world.

Does that mean every kid needs to be kicked out?

I’ll be the first to admit that I think about this differently for sons and daughters. My wife is pregnant with our baby girl, and we have a young son, Elijah. I can imagine telling my son it’s time to move out at 18. When I think about my daughter, I don’t know if I have it in me to send an 18-year-old girl out on the street.

Call me sexist. I’ll admit I’m thinking about their safety differently. But I also think there are ways to get some of the benefits of pushing your kids out of the nest without throwing them to the wolves.

And I recognize that families and cultures are different. My excitement about moving out and being independent comes from a particular American experience. Not every family sees living together the same way, and circumstances can make moving out much harder for some people than others.

Charge them rent

If your adult child stays under your roof, charge them rent. Set rules and boundaries that weren’t applicable when they were a child. They’re paying for a place to live and contributing toward the food going into their belly.

I know the idea of a landlord-and-tenant relationship with your kid might scare some parents. You don’t want to lose the parent-child relationship. But that relationship isn’t going away just because you establish adult expectations in your home.

The Bible says, “If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat” (2 Thessalonians 3:10, ESV). The passage talks about people who are unwilling to work; I’m not talking about someone who is unable to work.

I believe you are hurting your child if you keep giving them freebies without expecting responsibility. They can become lazy and spoiled, and as a parent, you have helped train that. You may think they just need more time to mature, but what have you taught them to do?

I’ve seen friends from high school go to college on their parents’ dime or take out loans, then come home with debt and keep living at home. Some of them, from what I’ve observed, are refusing to grow up. Meanwhile, some of the most successful friends I know got jobs, moved out, and took responsibility for their finances.

From what I’ve seen, time alone doesn’t teach responsibility. You have to give people the chance to practice it.

Parents, look in the mirror

You know the question: are there bad dogs, or just bad dog owners? I believe there aren’t bad dogs, only bad owners. I know it’s weird to compare children to dogs, but here’s why I bring it up: the person responsible for teaching and setting expectations matters.

You are an authority figure in your children’s lives. You’ve taught them almost everything they know, including things you never meant to teach them. They observe your actions throughout their lives.

If your child is spoiled, look in the mirror before you look for someone else to blame. You can share that responsibility with your spouse, whether they’re present in your child’s life or not. The habits you model and the expectations you set matter.

This is why I want parents to think about preparing their kids for adulthood, not simply waiting for adulthood to arrive. Teach them what it costs to live. Teach them to work, to contribute, and to take care of themselves.

Make a living doing what you love

I understand that America is changing. Moving out can be difficult and scary, and it seems harder to find entry-level jobs. To me, one way forward is to create your own products and services to offer others.

That’s why I created Free Market Wildlife: to help people work toward making a full-time career out of what they love.

Whether you’re 12, 18, 32, 65, or any age, you can work toward a career doing something you love and building an income that supports you. Start learning, start contributing, and start creating something of your own.
