## Intro

### Page 1 — Do you take God at His Word?

Before we begin, I want to make my purpose absolutely clear. My purpose is to help you know God. We are aliens in a foreign land. And whether we realize it or not, we bring assumptions about God from the way we grew up and the culture around us. Every misconception His Word corrects is a blessing. Every correction helps us know Him better.

### Page 2 — Why I place my understanding under Scripture

Honestly, it shocks me how readily we can place animal suffering inside the original world God called very good. But I’m not saying that disagreeing with me about creation means you don’t belong to Christ.

I am asking us to examine what we believe, and why. The authority I’m speaking with today is not my own. I don’t want you to believe something just because I say it. I want us to let Scripture interpret Scripture.

### Page 3 — Context shapes a plain reading (1 of 2)

Taking Jesus seriously does not mean looking for hinges when He calls Himself the door. In John 10 He uses a shepherd-and-sheep image to explain entry and salvation through Him. The surrounding speech tells us how to understand the image.

### Page 4 — Context shapes a plain reading (2 of 2)

That is how I want to approach Genesis too: read the passage in its setting, notice its genre, and compare related passages. The presence of figurative language in one passage does not, by itself, decide the genre or chronology of another. My interpretation remains accountable to the text.

## Evolution Worldview

### Page 5 — Evolution is real.

Evolution is a proven, observable, undeniable fact of God's creation. Now, before anybody panics, what do I mean? Heritable characteristics of populations change across generations. We observe variation, inheritance and natural selection. I believe the God who made creatures made them capable of adaptation.

I’m also part of the catholic church. Did you guys know that? What do we mean in the Apostles’ Creed by the holy catholic church? Universal. Where does your head go? Often to the Roman Catholic Church. Terminology matters. We need to know what claim we are actually talking about.

### Page 6 — Different words, different questions (1 of 2)

When I spoke with Ben about evolution, he thought first about stars and planets. Someone else hears the same word and pictures human ancestry. Those are different subjects. Cosmic development concerns the history of the universe. Biological evolution concerns changes in living populations and the ancestry of organisms. Abiogenesis concerns the origin of the first life.

### Page 7 — Different words, different questions (2 of 2)

Philosophical naturalism makes a further claim about reality and the absence of a Creator. These subjects can connect in someone’s worldview, but they are not interchangeable. A Christian who accepts common ancestry has not therefore said that God does not exist. I disagree with that Christian’s reading of creation, but I must represent the position accurately.

### Page 10 — The question underneath the debate (1 of 2)

Frank Turek and I agree on a lot here. My central disagreement is not a number for the age of Earth. It is when animal death enters the story, and what God calls very good. I do not place animal death before the completed sixth day. God’s power is not the limitation. He did not need six days, six seconds, or billions of years.

### Page 11 — The question underneath the debate (2 of 2)

When I picture animal starvation and disease before God calls His completed creation very good, I want to ask whether that fits the account. Those two words need the food provision and wider biblical context; they are not a technical biology definition. We’re about to take a journey through the Bible. This is a devotional on God’s Word, so let’s focus on His Word.

## Biblical Worldview

### Page 12 — The Creator is the Redeemer (1 of 2)

Where would you start? At the beginning. When would you start taking God at His Word? Book one, chapter one, verse one. Genesis 1:1 opens by identifying God as Creator. Do you believe it? Okay, let’s move on. God is not one more thing inside the universe.

### Page 14 — Six days. A completed, very good creation. (1 of 2)

I read Genesis as six ordinary days. The numbered days, repeated evenings and mornings, and Exodus’s creation-workweek connection matter to my reading. But I want my central point clear: I am not trying to prove a numerical age for Earth today. I am asking what belongs in the completed creation God calls very good.

### Page 16 — One ordered creation week (1 of 2)

Notice the correspondences between the first three days and the next three. Light and darkness correspond with the lights governing day and night. Waters and sky correspond with sea creatures and birds. Land and vegetation correspond with land animals and humanity. These pairings help us pay attention to the account’s order.

### Page 18 — Vegetation on day three; food on day six (1 of 2)

Genesis 1:9–13 places land and vegetation bearing seed and fruit on day three. The food assignment quoted here comes later, at the end of day six. Verse 29 addresses the people God has made.

I love the provision in that picture: plants, seed and fruit given for food. A fruit tree can feed someone and continue growing, bearing again in another season. I want to notice that provision without mixing up the days or the subjects of the verses.

### Page 19 — Food for the creatures described (2 of 2)

The next verse addresses the beasts, birds and the other creatures described as having the breath of life. Animals can disperse some seeds after eating fruit, but that varies by species.

I am not saying every seed survives every animal or that no plant cells die. Plants are living organisms. My theological question concerns animal suffering and death in the world God calls very good, and that wider conclusion still needs an argument.

### Page 20 — Very good (1 of 2)

Here is the assessment I keep returning to: very good. God looks at everything He has made and approves His completed creation. What belongs inside that declaration? I believe it is difficult to place animal starvation, disease, predation, and death before human rebellion and then call that the means by which God established His original good world.

### Page 22 — Finished work. A day of rest. (1 of 2)

Do not let the chapter number stop you. Genesis 2:1–3 completes the creation account. God finishes His work, ceases from it and blesses the seventh day. The rest marks completion. Exodus draws on this pattern for Israel’s work and Sabbath.

I appreciate that God gives His people a rhythm. We are creatures who work and need rest. His completed creation gives us something to receive with gratitude rather than treating life as an endless attempt to prove everything depends on us.

### Page 25 — The command before disobedience (1 of 4)

Genesis gives the man a command before the account of rebellion. He may eat from the garden’s trees, with a prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and a warning about death.

Read this warning in its own place in the story. It establishes human accountability to the Creator. The issue is not that people lacked enough information to win a debate. They were responsible to hear and obey God.

### Page 26 — Human rebellion in the narrative (2 of 4)

Genesis 3 then describes taking and eating the prohibited fruit. The narrative presents disobedience, not merely an intellectual misunderstanding. I want us to hear that moral claim. It reaches beyond whether we are skilled at explaining creation.

We stand before the Creator as people accountable to Him. The following judgments belong to the account’s response to that transgression, so we should read the action and its consequences in sequence.

### Page 27 — Cursed ground and painful toil (3 of 4)

The judgment concerning the man names his disobedience and the ground’s curse. Painful toil changes the way we understand the world we inhabit. Our present experience is not simply the original creation continuing untouched.

There is beauty and provision, but also pain and rupture. This is one of the narrative connections I bring into the larger theological argument. I must still distinguish what this judgment actually states from a complete chronology of every creature’s suffering.

### Page 28 — The return to dust (4 of 4)

The man is told that he will return to the ground from which he was taken. That human return to dust matters to the Bible’s later discussion of Adam and Christ.

I want us to pay attention to the connection between human rebellion and our mortality without claiming that Genesis supplies every biological date we might ask for. The narrative’s judgment is serious, and it prepares us to hear why humanity needs redemption.

### Page 29 — Adam and Christ (1 of 2)

Romans 5 connects sin entering through one man with death spreading to humanity. Paul is explaining our relationship to Adam and our need for Christ. This passage matters enormously to the gospel. Adam’s disobedience and Christ’s obedience are not disconnected ideas. But notice the precision: Romans 5 directly discusses human sin and human death.

### Page 31 — Creation groans and waits (1 of 2)

Romans 8 widens our attention to creation itself. Paul describes futility, groaning, and the hope of liberation from corruption. He connects that hope with the revealing of God’s children and the redemption of our bodies. That gives us a way to look honestly at suffering without treating it as the last truth about the world.

### Page 34 — My central question about animal death (2 of 2)

But Genesis does not print a timetable for every species. Romans 5 directly addresses human sin and death, not a separate explicit statement that every animal was immortal. Romans 8 gives groaning and hope, not a detailed biology chronology. We need to identify that interpretive step honestly. A numerical age for the planet is not the test I am setting.

### Page 35 — Judgment and mercy in Noah’s story (1 of 2)

Read the biblical Flood narrative in its setting. Genesis 6–9 concerns human wickedness, judgment, divine preservation and covenant. Jesus uses Noah’s days in Matthew 24, and Peter refers to creation, Flood and judgment. These passages make the opening narrative significant within Christian teaching.

## Why does this matter?

### Page 37 — What does this say about God? (1 of 2)

We have reached the heart of why this matters to me. What does our account of creation say about the God who made it? Scripture reveals His holiness, goodness, mercy, justice, wisdom, and love. I do not want to invent a version of goodness that simply mirrors my preferences. I want to ask how the Creator reveals Himself.

### Page 39 — Admiration and final authority

I admire C. S. Lewis. That does not make every conclusion he reached my final authority. In The Problem of Pain he considers animal suffering before humanity, including speculative prehuman evil. He took the problem seriously; I still disagree with his approach.

Augustine also reminds us that different readings of creation days predate Darwin. Admiration does not settle interpretation. Not Lewis. Not Augustine. Not your pastor. And certainly not me. Bring the argument back to Scripture. Chronological snobbery cuts both ways: older does not automatically mean false, and newer does not automatically mean wiser.

### Page 40 — Three questions about death (1 of 2)

Am I saying every death in this fallen world is a moral evil? No. God’s judgment is righteous. Ezekiel calls the wicked to turn and live and says God takes no pleasure in their death.

We can affirm justice without celebrating death itself as our final good. Genesis 9 also permits animals for food. That is a different question from cruelty, and a different question from original creation.

### Page 41 — Three questions about death (2 of 2)

Here are the distinctions I want us to keep: what belonged to the world God originally called very good, what God justly judges after rebellion, and what He permits for food in the fallen world.

Keeping these separate strengthens the argument rather than weakening it. You can’t eat an In-N-Out burger without the death of a cow, but eating it is not therefore a sin. That is different from my question about original creation.

### Page 42 — The question turns toward us (1 of 4)

My main purpose is to help you understand what God means when He says good. Have you heard the story of the rich young ruler? Now I want this to get personal.

It is possible to defend six days passionately and still resist what God says about your own sin. The man in Mark wants eternal life and addresses Jesus as good teacher. Jesus turns his attention toward God’s unique goodness. The question reaches beyond winning an argument about the beginning.

### Page 44 — Will he follow Jesus? (3 of 4)

Jesus looks at him and loves him, then calls him to a costly act of trust and to follow. The man leaves sorrowful because he has great possessions. The issue reaches his allegiance. What does he prize more than following Christ?

I want that question to reach me too. God’s goodness is not a way to congratulate myself. It exposes the depth of my need and asks what I am unwilling to place under His authority.

### Page 45 — God knows the public and private self (4 of 4)

You know your failures, and God knows your failures. He knows the public version and the private version. He knows selfishness beneath an impressive act and bitterness I keep explaining away. Psalm 139 describes the speaker as already searched and known by God.

I cannot argue my way out of that knowledge. Neither can you. Romans 3:23 says all have sinned and fall short of God’s glory. We need more than a better public image.

## Conclusion

### Page 46 — Christ died for our sins (1 of 2)

This is more important than the creationist thing. More important than the six days. It is not about winning a science argument. It is about the gospel. Jesus lived the righteous life we have not lived. Mark 10:45 describes His giving His life as a ransom for many.

### Page 47 — Christ died for our sins (2 of 2)

First Peter 3:18 describes Christ suffering for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring us to God. Second Corinthians 5:21 brings His sinlessness and our righteousness in Him together. This is the destination of the devotional. Our need is deeper than an improved argument about origins: we need reconciliation with the God against whom we have sinned.

### Page 48 — Christ died and rose (1 of 2)

Paul’s Gospel includes Christ’s death for sins, burial and resurrection. He is reminding the Corinthians of the message he received and delivered to them. The resurrection is essential to that message. It is not a decorative ending to a creation talk.

Jesus’ death concerns our sins, and the risen Christ is the foundation of our hope. I want this discussion to lead toward worship of Him, rather than stop with an argument about the past.

### Page 50 — A gift, not a wage we earn (1 of 4)

Salvation is not your reward for understanding every chapter of Genesis. It is God’s gift of grace received through faith in Christ. Sin’s wages and God’s gift are different.

I cannot purchase eternal life by church attendance, a good reputation or winning a debate. Christ is the ground of my hope. That is why the conclusion of this devotional matters more than whether you agree with my creation chronology.

### Page 51 — Repent and trust Christ (2 of 4)

If you have never turned to Him, repent and trust Him. Do not rest your hope in your own goodness or your ability to defend a position. Rest it in Christ, crucified and risen. Repentance is not merely saying that somebody else is wrong.

It concerns turning from our sin toward God. Faith is not a polished religious performance; its object is Christ and His Gospel. A familiar invitation phrase must not replace what Jesus actually calls for.

### Page 52 — Accepted in Christ, still confessing sin (3 of 4)

If you belong to Him, your acceptance rests in Christ. God does not become unaware of His children’s sins. We still confess them and seek His cleansing. My earlier rough wording that He does not see our wickedness needed that correction.

No condemnation does not mean that we are already flawless or that sin has become invisible to God. Justification and our continuing growth in holiness must be distinguished.

### Page 53 — Good works grow from grace (4 of 4)

Good works do not become the price we pay to purchase salvation. They grow from God’s grace. Ephesians places the exclusion of boasting beside the purpose of walking in good works.

So I must not use the rich man’s story to deny that a believer can do anything rightly called good. God’s sinless righteousness, a created thing’s goodness, and ordinary or Spirit-produced human good are related uses that require attention to context.

### Page 54 — The curse will not remain (1 of 2)

Our hope reaches beyond forgiveness alone. Christ will raise His people. God will dwell with them. Death, mourning, and the curse will not rule forever. The promised future is not merely that we stop asking questions about the past. It is life with our Creator in a world no longer broken by sin.

### Page 56 — Submit yourself to God’s Word (1 of 2)

Here is the response I am asking for. Submit yourself to God’s Word. Bring Him the belief you have never questioned. Bring Him the command you keep avoiding. Bring Him the part of your life where you want forgiveness but resist His authority. Do not take my word for everything we have discussed. Open Genesis.

### Page 57 — Submit yourself to God’s Word (2 of 2)

Read the passages around the verses. Bring your questions. If I have handled a passage wrongly, show me. I need to submit to His Word too. Let us pray that God gives us humility, corrects our errors, deepens our trust in Christ, and makes us willing to obey. The question is whether we will hear Him and respond.
